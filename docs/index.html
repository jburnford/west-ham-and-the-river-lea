A PLACE TO WALK. A PLACE OF WORK.

West Ham’s “boulevard”

In 1902, J. J. Terrett mocked the idea of this sewer-top walk as a grand promenade. His description brought gasworks, chemical works and the pumping station into one uncomfortable view. This landscape asks a question that runs through Jim Clifford’s book: how did the industries serving a growing city shape life beside the river?

Based on West Ham and the River Lea, chapter 3. Terrett’s description was a political criticism, not the voice of every resident.

A working adaptation of Jim Clifford’s West Ham and the River Lea.

THE EVIDENCE & THE GAPS

Building the view

A location, not yet a photograph match

The bridge’s approximate listed grid reference, TQ 38900 83209, anchors the scene. You can move within a 48 × 11 metre rectangle inside the bridge walkway, starting near its south side. This is an exploration limit, not a measured historical bridge boundary. Its eye level, the deck alignment and all building dimensions are estimates for this sketch. We have not yet fitted the historic photographs to this camera.

A changing crossing

The bridge was rebuilt and widened in 1900–1902. Our working period spans that change; the simple deck shown here does not claim to depict either version precisely. Historic England: sewer bridge.

Maps give us the ground plan

River and industrial-site outlines come from the author’s supplied GIS. Housing rows and six West Ham gas-holder circles are traced from inspected scans of London VIII.32 and VIII.22, revised 1893 and published 1895. Their alignment uses approximate sheet bounds and still needs refinement. Mill Meads remains open, as the map shows. The brown river surface interprets exposed bed; it is not a measured low-water shoreline.

Buildings are still study models

The housing follows mapped row envelopes; its heights, repeated windows, chimneys and household divisions are interpretations informed by local photographs. Abbey Mills’ architecture draws on modern and period views. Factory buildings still need individual identification. Seven Bromley holders form the southern group; two lost holders are not yet modelled. Gas-bell levels, frame detail and colours remain estimates. Abbey Lane houses · Abbey Mills · Bromley gas holders.

The raised sewer, gardens and railways

The sewer follows mapped bends and continues in both directions. Its height follows the author’s description of a walkway near roof level; the bank profile and distant continuations are estimates. Rail routes follow sheets VIII.32 and VIII.42. The author identifies gardens south of the pumping station; individual beds and sheds are interpreted. Abbey Mill is shown at its mapped site, without assuming that the windmill pictured around 1800 survived into this scene.

Photographs guide the model

The author identifies different gasworks in the two views: the 1902 close-up looks toward West Ham Gas Works to the north; the wide 1900 view shows Bromley to the south. The exact photograph viewpoints remain to be matched. Barges, timber retaining edges, exposed mud, factory windows and surface colours in this study interpret the photographs; individual positions, dimensions and colours remain estimates. Modern photographs help with surviving details. No photograph textures are used in this sketch. The overcast light, industrial haze, weathered brick and discoloured water interpret the bleakness of the photographs and the book’s account of sewage and chemical industry; exact colours and pollution levels cannot be recovered from these views.
