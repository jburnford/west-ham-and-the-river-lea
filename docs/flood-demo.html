THE LOWER LEA · 1900 LANDSCAPE

Low ground, high water

Much of southern West Ham occupied former marshland below high-tide level. When the river overtopped its banks, water spread into low ground where it could collect and be slow to drain. Heavy rain could also overwhelm drainage.

This first experiment covers one small drainage crossing. It explores how local obstacles and outlets affect water movement; it does not yet model the wider basin, tidal rivers or riverbank overtopping. The embankments are one part of that larger story.

A local drainage experiment

Raise water in a ditch, block its connection, or add rain. These are controlled tests, not reconstructions of the historical floods. In particular, raising the western outlet tests backwater through a drain, not water flowing over a riverbank.

Rain, pumping and uncertain heights

The pump is an assumed extraction from the eastern drain, not an identified historical pump. Height changes move the ground, road, railway, channel beds and culvert together against the same water levels.

What this experiment establishes — and what it does not

The 540 × 160 m test area uses a 2 m grid. Both channel ends exchange water with prescribed reservoirs. The northern and southern edges are closed test boundaries; the western outlet is not a confirmed historical outfall. Water can cross the embankments on the surface if it reaches their crests, even with the culvert blocked.

The working culvert opening is 0.9 × 0.6 m over a 45 m candidate route. A one-way control permits east-to-west flow only when the water levels allow it. The model includes opening losses and resistance along the barrel; its capacity has not been calibrated.

The former constant railway height conflicts with a road bridge farther south. This experiment replaces it with a common local formation between 1.0 and 1.9 m ODN. The default permits about 3.46 m between rail top and the assumed bridge soffit there. This is a test range, not a surveyed railway profile.

The solver moves and accounts for water between cells. It includes wetting, drying, rainfall, boundary inflow/outflow, culvert exchange and the optional pump. It omits sewer networks, infiltration and fast-flow momentum effects. Flood timing and depths are illustrative until geometry and event conditions are calibrated.

Before claiming a reconstruction of 1888, 1897, 1904 or 1928, we still need event-specific infrastructure, local river levels and comparison with reported inundation. The Three Mills marks do not yet have established absolute heights in this model.

Method references: Bates, Horritt & Fewtrell: local-inertial flow; EPA SWMM hydraulic references. This is a separate demo implementation, not either of those software packages.
