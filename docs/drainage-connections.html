How could water cross the railway and Manor Road?

Our working reconstruction drains water east to west through a culvert beneath the embankment. Ditches on both sides, mapped sluices and nearby ground levels support that interpretation. Compare it with blocked or ineffective controls; the pipe dimensions and gate mechanism remain provisional.

Loading the connection model…

This is a connection study, not a flood prediction. It calculates no discharge, flood depth or timing. Trace boundaries are not confirmed outfalls. The disconnected northern sluice has no reviewed connecting trace.

What could fit beneath the road?

The road profile constrains the space available. These alternative culvert sections continue the assumed eastern drain bed towards the west. Their dimensions, gradient, 0.15 m roof and 0.25 m cover allowance are modelling assumptions, not recovered engineering specifications.

A downward bed slope permits gravity drainage only when water levels and gate operation allow it. The western outfall remains unresolved, and no flow capacity is assigned. Choosing a section here does not excavate the landscape or open a hydraulic connection.

The 1907 West Ham inquiry (p.29, note 1) describes lost open drainage and inadequate sewers and pumps after the 1888 flood. Chapter 6 of Jim Clifford’s West Ham and the River Lea records pumping failures during floods. Neither source establishes this culvert’s dimensions.

The 1848–51 skeleton map is too sparse here to establish continuity or disappearance of these field drains. These scenarios apply to the 1900 reconstruction only.
