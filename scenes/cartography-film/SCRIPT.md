# The marsh that became a town

**A map-led film about work, water and everyday life in West Ham.** Draft 5, 30 September 2026. Approximately seven minutes, including pauses to look at the maps; final timing follows the voice read.

Begin with a flooded pumping station, go back to the marsh, then follow the arrival of docks, factories and housing. Return to the flood with an understanding of what was at stake. The maps supply the landscape, the surprises and the transitions.

Use the beautiful 1777 county sheet for the early landscape and the detailed 1890s plans for streets, factories and waterways. One short comparison with a complete, sharp 1860s crop shows the growth. There is no obligation to include every edition. Source and production notes follow the script; they are not narration.

---

## 1 · A fire engine for the pumping station

**Picture.** Start close enough to read streets beside Bow Creek on the 1890s plan. Follow the river for a few seconds, then move inland through the rows of houses. Reveal the title after the opening incident, not before it. Do not identify a particular pumping-station building: the location of the flooded local pump house is not established for this shot.

**On screen.** WEST HAM · THE FLOOD OF 1888. Smaller, separate map label: Landscape mapped in the 1890s.

**Narration.**

In eighteen eighty-eight, West Ham needed a fire engine to pump out a pumping station.

Floodwater had put out the fires that powered the pumps. The machinery meant to drain the town had itself been flooded.

Look at these streets beside the River Lea. Within a few decades, low marshland had become a place of factories, docks and thousands of homes. People came here for work. Keeping their homes dry became a constant struggle.

To see how this happened, we need to go back to a landscape with far fewer streets.

**Pause.** Let the last rows of houses hold briefly. Cut to the open marsh on the 1777 sheet.

## 2 · Before the streets

**Picture.** Begin wide on the 1777 sheet, with the Lea meeting the Thames. Move towards the engraved words *Plaistow Level*, then north to the settlements and mills. Give the hand colouring, river bends and lettering time on screen. A restrained highlight follows the river; it does not replace the original drawing.

**On screen.** 1777 · THE MARSHES EAST OF LONDON

**Narration.**

The Thames runs along the bottom of this landscape. The Lea comes down from the north, splitting into channels before it reaches the larger river.

Much of the ground between them is marsh. But this is already a working landscape. Farmers have drained fields and built banks to keep the water out. Livestock graze here before being taken to market in London. Around Plaistow, growers produce potatoes for the city.

There is industry too. The tide helps drive the mills. Cloth printers work beside the waterways. At Three Mills, the name tells you what drew people to this spot: water could do work.

London is close enough to buy what this landscape produces. For now, fields still separate its small settlements. That is about to change.

## 3 · Coal comes up the river

**Picture.** Cut to the large, crisp dock basins on the 1890s plan. Caption the opening date of Victoria Dock separately from the map date. Trace one route from the Thames through Bow Creek and towards the factory wharves. Then find a short stretch where a siding, a works and a waterside yard can be seen together.

**On screen.** VICTORIA DOCK OPENS · 1855. Map label remains: 1890s.

**Narration.**

Victoria Dock opened in eighteen fifty-five. Its great basin was cut into the low ground beside the Thames.

One of the most important things arriving here was coal. Ships brought it to the docks. Barges carried it onwards to factories along the Lea.

Steam power did not make the river obsolete. The river supplied the fuel.

Follow these waterways and the factory sites begin to make sense. A wharf gave a works a place to unload. A railway siding offered another route in and out. Cheap transport helped make West Ham an attractive place to manufacture goods for London and beyond.

The water that had turned mill wheels now helped feed a much larger industrial economy.

## 4 · What London bought

**Picture.** Three deliberate close-ups: a soapworks, a sugar refinery and a gasworks. Allow each original label to become readable before adding a short modern caption. At Abbey Marsh, use a clean 1860s/1890s comparison to reveal additional buildings, then return to the later map. End on gardens beside the Bromley gasworks.

**On screen.** SOAP · SUGAR · GAS. Introduce each word with its own shot.

**Narration.**

Some of these factory names belong to ordinary daily life: soap, sugar, gas for lighting and cooking. Others made chemicals, rubber and telegraph cable.

The things people bought and used elsewhere had to be made somewhere. Here, that meant coal deliveries, furnaces, waste and smoke, often close to the houses of the people doing the work.

Factories spread along the riverbanks. Older industrial grounds filled with new buildings. Yet look beside this gasworks: there is still cultivated land.

West Ham did not turn from green countryside into a solid block of factories overnight. Gardens, workshops, large works and open marsh existed alongside one another. A short walk could take someone from a crowded street to a field, with a gas holder rising beyond it.

## 5 · A town follows the jobs

**Picture.** Travel from the industrial waterfront into Canning Town's terraces. Hold on one small block, then pull back to reveal how often that pattern repeats. Two population figures appear in succession, over the same steady view. Move south towards Silvertown for the strike passage. Do not animate invented crowds or assign occupations to individual houses.

**On screen.** WEST HAM: ABOUT 19,000 PEOPLE IN 1851 → 267,000 IN 1901. Later: STRIKES · 1889.

**Narration.**

Work brought people here in enormous numbers. In eighteen fifty-one, West Ham had about nineteen thousand residents. Fifty years later, it had more than a quarter of a million.

New housing spread onto the marsh. Some early builders followed the drainage ditches, putting homes along their banks. Channels dug to carry water away became open sewers beside the new streets.

Families needed wages from the docks and factories. They also needed clean water, better drainage and homes they could afford. The growth of industry did not guarantee steady work or healthy surroundings.

These districts became centres of labour organisation. Many of the dockers who struck in eighteen eighty-nine lived and worked in southern West Ham. Gasworkers organised too, and workers at the rubber and telegraph works in Silvertown went on strike that summer.

Behind these rows of little rectangles were people trying to improve the terms on which they lived and worked.

## 6 · The marsh is still underneath

**Picture.** Return to drainage channels on Plaistow Level, then to the low streets beside Bow Creek. Follow a ditch towards a sluice and stop. Use a few restrained arrows to distinguish rainfall draining from the land from tidal water approaching up the river. They indicate directions and pressures, not a reconstructed flood extent. During the pumping-station account, stay with the affected district rather than cutting to Abbey Mills.

**On screen.** DRAINING THE TOWN

**Narration.**

Building streets had not raised all this land beyond the reach of water.

Rain had to drain away. River walls had to hold. When water collected on the low ground, pumps helped remove it. Those pumps depended on coal and on fires that had to keep burning.

That brings us back to eighteen eighty-eight. Floodwater stopped the pumps when they were needed most. A fire engine had to drain the pump house before it could do its job again.

Residents demanded better drainage. The borough took on greater responsibility for sewers and flood defences, but maintaining a town on this ground was expensive. It also required action by people and organisations with different interests in the rivers.

Flooding returned in eighteen ninety-seven and nineteen-oh-four. In nineteen twenty-eight, another severe flood spread through houses, factories and wharves. The waterways that brought work into West Ham could also interrupt it, damage homes and destroy possessions.

## 7 · Work to change the rivers

**Picture.** Follow the back rivers on the 1890s plan. Add a simple, clearly labelled annotation locating the subsequent 1930s works. Keep the historic ink visible; do not erase rivers and present the altered picture as a later map. Return to Three Mills, then finish with the 1777 marsh and the 1890s town side by side.

**On screen.** THE 1930s · FLOOD RELIEF AND JOBS. For the annotation: Later works shown on an earlier map.

**Narration.**

In the nineteen-thirties, flood relief and unemployment became part of the same project. Public money paid for major changes to the waterways, and jobless men were employed to carry them out.

Some channels were filled in. Others were moved. Bridges were rebuilt and the High Street widened. Several old mills were demolished. Three Mills survived, with a new flood channel running nearby.

Return to the earlier marsh and the scale of the change is striking. Docks have been dug into fields. Factories crowd the waterways. Streets reach across ground that once supported grazing and market gardens.

London gained fuel, manufactured goods and places to work. The people who made their homes here also had to fight for better wages, healthier streets and protection from floods.

They had built a town on the marsh. Keeping it habitable was work that never ended.

**End.** Hold the two maps for four seconds. Fade to a brief source credit. No book cover, software demonstration or promotional sign-off.

### Pace and clarity

- Give each shot one discovery: a factory name, a dock basin, a row of houses or a drainage channel. Hold a readable detail for a few seconds before moving.
- Alternate wider views and close details. Use a cut when changing subject; reserve a slow move for following a route or revealing scale.
- Let the maps remain visible. Use short captions, one highlight at a time and two restrained date comparisons. No continuous timeline scrubbing.
- Keep map dates visible when telling an earlier event over a later sheet. The 1890s map supplies context for the 1855 dock opening, the 1888 flood and the 1889 strikes; it is not a map made in those years.
- If using ambient sound, keep it quiet beneath the voice and pauses. Do not add invented eyewitness speech or turn the 1888 pump-house incident into a staged scene.
- The two population numbers are the only substantial statistical graphic. Speak the dates naturally; map credits can retain numerals.
